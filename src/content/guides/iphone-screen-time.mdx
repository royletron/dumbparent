## Before you start

Apple Screen Time is built into every iPhone running iOS 12 or later. It lets
you set content filters, daily app limits, and communication controls. When
combined with **Family Sharing**, you can manage it all from your own iPhone
without needing to touch your child's phone.

This guide is written for parents of **teenagers (11–17)**. For younger children
with an iPad, the process is similar but we recommend stricter settings.

<Callout type="info" title="What you'll need">
- Your child's iPhone running iOS 16 or later (Settings → General → About)
- Your own iPhone or iPad signed in to the same Apple Family Sharing group
- Your child's Apple ID credentials — or help setting one up
- About 25 minutes
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Have the conversation first">
  Teenagers often feel that Screen Time is surveillance rather than safety.
  Before you set this up, explain why you're doing it and what you will and
  won't be able to see. Agreeing on limits together leads to much better outcomes
  than setting them without discussion.
</Callout>

## Step 1 — Set up Family Sharing (if not already done)

<Steps>
  <Step title="On your own iPhone, open Settings and tap your name">
    Your Apple ID settings appear at the top of Settings (below the search bar).
    Tap your name, then tap **Family Sharing**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Add your child to your Family group">
    Tap **Add Family Member** → **Create Child Account** (if your child doesn't
    have an Apple ID) or **Invite** (if they already do).

    If creating an account for a child under 13, Apple will require your consent
    as the parent.
  </Step>
</Steps>

---

## Step 2 — Turn on Screen Time

<Steps>
  <Step title="On your child's iPhone, go to Settings → Screen Time">
    Scroll down in Settings until you see **Screen Time** (below the Do Not
    Disturb section). Tap it.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Tap 'Turn On Screen Time'">
    A short explanation screen appears. Tap **Continue**, then select
    **This is my child's iPhone**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Set a Screen Time passcode">
    Choose a 4-digit passcode that **only you know**. This prevents your child
    from changing the Screen Time settings.

    <Callout type="warning">
      Do not use a passcode your child knows, such as your device passcode or
      their birthday. This is the single most important step — if they know the
      passcode, all other restrictions can be removed.
    </Callout>
  </Step>
</Steps>

---

## Step 3 — Block inappropriate content

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open Content & Privacy Restrictions">
    In Screen Time settings, tap **Content & Privacy Restrictions** and turn
    the toggle **on** at the top.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Set app and content ratings">
    Tap **Content Restrictions** and review each category:

    | Category | Recommended setting for 13+ |
    |---|---|
    | Music, Podcasts & News | Explicit content: Off |
    | Movies | 15 (or 12 for younger teens) |
    | TV Shows | 15 |
    | Books | Off (blocks explicit books) |
    | Apps | 12+ or 17+ depending on age |
    | Web Content | Limit Adult Websites |
    | Siri | Explicit language: Off |

  </Step>

  <Step title="Restrict web content">
    Under **Content Restrictions → Web Content**, choose **Limit Adult Websites**.
    This blocks most adult sites while keeping general browsing available.

    For stricter control, choose **Allowed Websites Only** — but be aware this
    will frustrate a teenager who needs to do homework research.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Block app installation and deletion" optional>
    In **Content & Privacy → iTunes & App Store Purchases**:
    - Set **Installing Apps** to **Don't Allow**
    - Set **Deleting Apps** to **Don't Allow**

    This means your child cannot install new apps or delete apps (which some
    use to hide content by deleting then reinstalling).

    <Callout type="tip">
      Instead of blocking installs entirely, you can enable **Ask to Buy** via
      Family Sharing. Your child can request an app; you get a notification and
      can approve or decline.
    </Callout>
  </Step>
</Steps>

---

## Step 4 — Set app time limits

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open App Limits in Screen Time">
    Back in **Screen Time**, tap **App Limits → Add Limit**.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Choose categories to limit">
    You can limit by category (e.g. **Social Networking**, **Entertainment**)
    or by individual app. Tap a category, then set the daily time limit.

    Common settings:
    - Social Media: 1–2 hours per day
    - Games: 1 hour per day
    - Entertainment (streaming): 2 hours per day
  </Step>

  <Step title="Turn on Downtime">
    Tap **Downtime** and set a start and end time. During Downtime, only apps
    you specifically allow will work — everything else is greyed out.

    A common choice is **9:00 pm to 7:00 am** to encourage sleep.
  </Step>
</Steps>

---

## Step 5 — Manage from your own device

<Steps>
  <Step title="Check your child's usage from your iPhone">
    On your iPhone, go to **Settings → Screen Time** and tap your child's name
    under Family. You can see a summary of their daily usage and adjust limits
    remotely — without needing their phone.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What your child can access

<WhatToExpect>
  <CanDo>Browse the web (with adult content filtering applied)</CanDo>
  <CanDo>Use approved apps within their daily time limits</CanDo>
  <CanDo>Make and receive calls and messages</CanDo>
  <CanDo>Request app downloads (if Ask to Buy is enabled)</CanDo>
  <CannotDo>Change Screen Time settings without your passcode</CannotDo>
  <CannotDo>Install apps without your approval (if restricted)</CannotDo>
  <CannotDo>Access explicitly rated content in Apple's ecosystem</CannotDo>
  <CannotDo>Use apps outside of permitted hours (during Downtime)</CannotDo>
</WhatToExpect>

## Side doors to watch out for

<Callout type="danger" title="Side door: Screen Time can be turned off with a factory reset">
  If a teenager performs a factory reset (Settings → General → Transfer or
  Reset iPhone → Erase All Content) and sets the phone up as a new device,
  Screen Time is removed.

  **What to do:** Make sure they cannot do this without your help — under
  **Content & Privacy → Allowed Changes**, set **Passcode Changes** to
  **Don't Allow**. Note that a determined teenager can still contact Apple
  Support if they know their Apple ID.
</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="Side door: VPNs bypass web content filtering">
  A teenager who installs a VPN app can bypass web content restrictions
  entirely.

  **What to do:** Block the installation of new apps, or check regularly for
  VPN apps. In Content & Privacy, set **VPN configurations** to **Don't Allow**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="Side door: A second device">
  Screen Time only controls the one device. A second phone, a friend's device,
  or a games console with a browser gives unrestricted access.

  **What to do:** This is a family conversation rather than a technical fix.
  Talk about expectations around other devices and what is and isn't okay.
</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="Side door: iMessage and AirDrop">
  Screen Time does not filter the content of iMessages, and AirDrop can be
  used to receive files from strangers in public places.

  **What to do:** In **Screen Time → Communication Limits**, you can restrict
  who your child can call and message. For AirDrop, go to **Settings →
  General → AirDrop** and set it to **Contacts Only** or **Receiving Off**.
</Callout>

## Tips for ongoing management

- **Check the weekly report** — Screen Time sends you a summary every Sunday.
  Use it as a prompt to discuss what your teen has been spending time on.
- **Be consistent** — if you set a 1-hour games limit, stick to it. Caving
  regularly teaches that the limits can be negotiated around.
- **Revisit limits as they get older** — what's right for a 13-year-old isn't
  right for a 16-year-old. Set a reminder to review settings every six months.
- **Include your teen in the process** — showing them what you can see builds
  trust. Many teenagers are less bothered by monitoring when they feel it's
  transparent rather than secretive.
